Swansea Women's Aid: a research-led redesign

A full redesign for a local domestic abuse charity, built around three audiences with very different needs — and very different amounts of time to find what they need.

The brief

A site that works for three audiences at once — women seeking help, often in crisis; donors and funders; and volunteers — with staff able to post updates themselves.

The research

Stakeholder interviews with the charity's team, user research commissioned through an external partner, and a benchmark against Refuge's national site.

The redesign

Navigation rebuilt around the three priority audiences, crisis support first. A visible "quick exit" safety feature, plain-language guidance on recognising abuse, and a lightweight CMS for the team to publish without developer help.

The outcome

Live today at swanseawomensaid.com, structured around what someone actually needs the moment they land: support first, then ways to give or get involved.

View the live site →

Key research findings

  • Many women weren't sure whether what they were experiencing qualified as abuse.
  • The existing site was hard to navigate, with no clear structure per audience.
  • Staff had no way to update content without developer support.

● Updated September 2026

Continued iteration: a full UX review and refresh

The site had been rebuilt in a hurry after an accidental content loss, so parts were never finished. I went back through it: fixing what hadn't been properly rebuilt, tightening the design, reorganising key journeys in plain English, and completing a full WCAG 2.2 AA accessibility pass, while keeping the charity's existing branding.

What changed in this round

  • A rebuilt, back-button-proof safety exit, and a Get help page with two clear actions.
  • Navigation and services reorganised in plain English, replacing internal acronyms.
  • A full WCAG 2.2 AA pass, plus a Welsh and community-languages menu.
  • Consistent design across desktop and mobile, with compressed images for faster loading.
  • CMS and forms fixed, including a Get involved form that reaches the team.

Sober Points: finding sober-friendly places

Product design · UX research · Interaction design · Prototyping · Usability testing

Sober Points helps people find places where choosing not to drink doesn't mean compromising on the experience. A venue might technically offer an alcohol-free option, but that doesn't make it a good experience for someone who isn't drinking. I founded the product and led its design from early research through to a live web platform and native app.

Narrowing a broad idea into one job

Early concepts explored a much broader sober-living ecosystem — communities, podcasts, events — before research progressively narrowed the proposition. Usability testing repeatedly surfaced the same thing: people didn't want another community platform, they wanted a fast, trustworthy answer to "where can I go near me?" That finding cut the product back to a focused discovery tool, mapped end-to-end rather than designed screen by screen.

Designing a two-sided service

The map and venue experience progressively reveals information: search by location, filter results, then open a venue profile with alcohol-free options and community reviews. Because it depends on the quality of venue data behind it, I designed contribution, moderation and venue-claiming journeys alongside the consumer experience, then used product analytics and qualitative research after launch to prioritise what to fix next. One recurring lesson: venue type and venue attribute don't belong at the same level, and treating them as equivalent filters created a confusing hierarchy as the dataset grew — separating them was a small change that showed how the information architecture has to evolve as a product matures.

From web product to native app

As the product developed, I translated the experience into a native mobile app — not a visual port of the website, but a rethink of map behaviour, navigation, authentication, saved venues and deep links. It's now a live product with hundreds of sober-friendly venues and real users searching and contributing. Building my own product meant owning decisions about user needs, data quality and commercial viability, then seeing how those decisions performed through an ongoing loop of research, design and iteration.
